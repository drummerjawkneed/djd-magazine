A fan-funded campaign raised $215,000 toward a memorial for Neil Peart at Lakeside Park — the actual park in St. Catharines, Ontario that gave Rush's 1975 song its name and its memories. It sat short of its $1M goal until Geddy Lee and Alex Lifeson showed up this month with a check for $785,000, drawn from VIP proceeds off the current tour, and closed the gap in one afternoon. That's not a press-release gesture. That's two guys deciding their old bandmate's hometown gets its due.

It's also the reason this issue exists in a slightly different shape than the last one. We're adding two new departments starting now: a Drummer Spotlight that goes back in time instead of forward — real history, not just this month's news — and a Pick of the Month, one specific piece of drumming we think deserves your ten minutes, argued for directly instead of buried in a roundup. Both start this issue with subjects that earned the space on their own merits, not because the format needed filling.

Meanwhile the actual touring keeps happening. Anika Nilles and Loren Gold aren't holding down a legacy act on autopilot — Rush just added four more arena dates through November because the band, in their own words, didn't expect to still be having this much fun on the road. Go hit something.

Cover Story

Rush Completes the Neil Peart Memorial

SourceLoudersound · September 23, 2026

The campaign to build a permanent Neil Peart memorial at Lakeside Park in St. Catharines, Ontario had been running on community donations for a while, and it had raised a real $215,000 — but it needed $1 million to actually happen. This month, Rush closed the rest of it in one move: Geddy Lee and Alex Lifeson presented the Neil Peart Commemorative Task Force with a check for $785,000, funded by a portion of VIP-experience proceeds from the band's own Fifty Something tour.

The memorial's design is two bronze statues, not one: Peart as a young musician holding a book and drumsticks, and Peart in his later years, cradling a snare drum. It's a deliberate arc — student to master, in the same park his own 1975 lyric named. "Lakeside Park," off Caress of Steel, was Peart writing about his actual childhood summers there before he ever played a note of it on a Rush record. The memorial is expected to open late 2027.

Worth sitting with: this is a working band, mid-tour, taking real box-office revenue and redirecting it into a monument for a bandmate who's been gone since 2020. That's not how legacy usually gets funded in rock. Usually it's a museum wing paid for by a corporation, or nothing at all. This was two guys with a checkbook, closing a gap their fans started.

Drummer Spotlight · Historical · New Department
Neil Peart
September 12, 1952 – January 7, 2020 · Rush, 1974–2015

Neil Peart joined Rush in 1974, right before their first US tour, and immediately took over the band's lyric-writing along with the drum chair — a dual role almost nobody else in rock history has held for an entire career. He wrote about science fiction, Ayn Rand-adjacent individualism he'd later walk back publicly, working-class dignity, and, on "Lakeside Park," his own hometown summers in St. Catharines, Ontario — the same park where his memorial is now being built.

His nickname, "The Professor," came from bandmates and fans both, for the same reason: a drummer who treated the instrument like something to be studied, not just felt. He took lessons well into his 40s and 50s, restructured his entire technique twice — first to incorporate more jazz vocabulary, later to build the stamina for Rush's increasingly demanding live shows. That's not typical for someone already considered one of the best to ever sit behind a kit.

He retired from touring in 2015 after Rush's R40 tour, citing tendonitis and the physical toll of the job — not a creative falling-out, just an honest read on what his body could still do. He died in January 2020 of glioblastoma, a brain cancer he'd kept private for over three years so he could keep touring without it becoming the story. Rush's decision to keep Anika Nilles and Loren Gold as full lineup members rather than hired guns, five years later, reads like the continuation of that same instinct: the work matters more than the myth around any one player.

Fifty Something Tour Adds Four More Arena Dates Through November
Rush's Fifty Something tour — already booked through April 2027 before this month — added four more stops: Houston (Toyota Center, October 1), St. Louis (Enterprise Center, October 21), Cincinnati (Heritage Bank Center, October 23), and Pittsburgh (PPG Paints Arena, November 15). Presales opened September 1, general tickets September 4. Geddy Lee and Alex Lifeson have been candid about the surprise of it: "we didn't know how well we would hold up to the rigours of the road," Lee said, "but life is nothing if not surprising and we're doing fine and having a blast." September itself was a packed month on the road — Montreal (Bell Centre, Sept 2 & 4), Boston (TD Garden, Sept 12 & 14), Cleveland (Rocket Mortgage Arena, Sept 17 & 19), and San Antonio (Frost Bank Center, Sept 23 & 25) — meaning the same week the band closed out the Peart memorial fund, they were also playing two-night stands four cities in a row. Anika Nilles and Loren Gold are fully load-bearing members of this lineup now, not guests.

Drummer Pick of the Month · New
Ilan Rubin's No-Click Foo Fighters Sessions
His first album with the band, cut live with Dave Grohl

Foo Fighters' twelfth album is Ilan Rubin's first as the band's drummer, and the detail worth caring about isn't the credit — it's the method. Rubin and Dave Grohl cut the rhythm tracks live, together, with no click track. In 2026, when most rock records get built on a rigid grid by default, that's a real choice with real risk: no safety net, no quantizing a rushed fill after the fact, just two players locking in on take after take until one of them was right.

This is our pick for exactly that reason — not because it's the flashiest drumming released this month, but because it's a real bet on human feel over studio convenience, made by a band with every resource available to do it the easier way instead. That's the kind of call we want to be pointing at every issue, not just covering after the fact.

The Community Funded a Statue Before the Industry Did
$215,000 of the Neil Peart memorial's budget came from ordinary fans, raised the slow way, before Rush's $785,000 closed the gap. No label, no streaming platform, no gear brand put up that money first. Look at who actually funds a drummer's legacy when nobody's required to: not the industry that profited off the work, but the people who showed up to watch it happen. That's not a knock on Rush, who clearly stepped up — it's a note on everyone else who didn't have to be asked twice.
02
Drum Culture · 02
No Click, No Net: What Ilan Rubin's Sessions Actually Risk
Cutting live with no click isn't a retro gimmick when a band with Foo Fighters' resources chooses it on purpose in 2026 — it's a bet that the take with the tiny human wobble in it is more valuable than the take that's perfectly on grid. Every home studio producer arguing over quantize settings should sit with the fact that two of the most successful rock musicians alive still choose the harder, riskier way when it's entirely optional. That's a real data point, not nostalgia.
03
Drum Culture · 03
Why This Issue Adds a Historical Department
Every drum-media outlet covers what's happening this week. Almost none of them regularly go backward on purpose. Neil Peart's own drumming only makes sense in the context of six decades of technique evolution most readers under 30 never got taught directly — the same is true for dozens of drummers who shaped what "good" even means on a kit. Streamer Scene covers who's building an audience right now. Drummer Spotlight is where we cover who already built the whole language they're building on.
04
Drum Culture · 04
A Legacy Drum Chair Isn't a Cover Gig
Five years after Peart's death, Rush is adding tour dates because the current lineup is working, not because nostalgia is selling tickets on autopilot. Anika Nilles didn't get handed a tribute gig — she got handed an actual drum chair in an actual band that still writes and tours new material around it. The distinction matters: one of those things freezes a legacy in amber, the other keeps it alive by changing hands to someone who can actually play it forward.
