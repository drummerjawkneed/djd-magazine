Ringo Starr Turns 86 at His Own Peace & Love Statue

SourceModern Drummer Magazine · July 9, 2026

On July 7th, Ringo Starr celebrated his 86th birthday the way he's celebrated it for years now — in public, at his Peace & Love Statue in Beverly Hills, surrounded by the people who've played alongside him. Current and former All Starr Band members joined the party, along with his wife Barbara Bach and sister-in-law Marjorie Walsh, as fans and friends came out to take photos and mark the occasion together.

The All Starr Band format is Ringo's own invention — a rotating cast of musicians touring under his name since 1989, which means the roster at any given birthday bash is basically a snapshot of who's been in his orbit lately. That's a different model than most legacy acts run, and it's part of why the birthday event reads less like a tribute concert and more like a reunion.

Ringo's been the drummer's drummer for over six decades — the guy who made pocket, feel, and serving the song more important than flash. An 86th birthday with a statue named after his own philosophy isn't just a milestone. It's proof the philosophy held up.

Rush's Fifty Something Tour Keeps Rolling — Nilles and Gold Still in the Chair
Let's be clear on the framing since it keeps getting garbled: Rush's Fifty Something tour marks roughly 50 years of the band, not a "50th anniversary tour," and it's a different beast entirely from the R40 tour that closed out August 1, 2015. The current lineup is Geddy Lee, Alex Lifeson, Anika Nilles on drums, and Loren Gold on keyboards — a lineup that's now been together long enough to stop being "the new Rush" and just be Rush. Whatever your Neil Peart nostalgia, Nilles has earned the seat on chops alone.

Anika Nilles at 43: Four Albums Deep and Still Building the Catalog
Born May 29, 1983, Anika Nilles is 43 as of this issue — and the discography backs up the resume: Pikalar (2017), For a Colorful Soul (2020), Opuntia (2022), and False Truth (2025). Four full-lengths under her own name, on top of holding down the drum chair for Rush. That's not a session player picking up a legacy gig — that's a bandleader who also happens to be playing arenas built by someone else's songbook.

Eleven Years Ago This Week, Rush Closed the Book on R40
August 1st marks eleven years since Rush's R40 tour wrapped in 2015 — the tour that was, at the time, framed as the band's farewell run. Eleven years, a lineup change, and a whole new tour cycle later, the band's still out there. Worth remembering the next time somebody tells you a "last tour" is actually the last tour.

The Simulcast Is the New Backline
Streaming to four platforms at once used to be a technical flex. Now it's just the setup. Twitch, Kick, YouTube, and X aren't competing destinations anymore for working streamers — they're redundancy, the same way a drummer keeps a backup snare in the car. No single platform's algorithm, TOS change, or outage should be able to end your night. DrummerJawkneeD's Tuesday/Thursday/Saturday simulcast isn't unusual anymore, and that's the point — the infrastructure question isn't "which platform" anymore, it's "why would you only pick one." The gear list for a serious drum streamer now includes OBS scene logic and multi-platform routing right next to the cymbals. That's not selling out the craft. That's just what showing up looks like in 2026.

86 and Still Behind the Kit Beats Any Farewell Tour
Ringo Starr didn't mark 86 with a retirement announcement. He marked it with a party at a statue he built about peace and love, surrounded by people he's actually played music with. Compare that to how many "final tour" cycles get announced and then quietly walked back two years later. There's something more honest about a guy who just keeps showing up instead of staging his own send-off. Longevity in drumming isn't about chops holding up forever — eventually they don't, for anyone. It's about still wanting to be in the room. Ringo clearly still wants to be in the room.

The Rotating Band Model Is Underrated
Ringo's All Starr Band has run on a rotating lineup since 1989 — different players, same format, same energy. Rush's current lineup swapping in Anika Nilles and Loren Gold works on a similar principle, just with higher stakes: replace a legend, don't just fill a chair. Both prove the same thing — a band's identity doesn't have to live in four unchangeable faces. It can live in a sound, a songbook, a standard. Musicians who treat lineup continuity as more sacred than the actual music are optimizing for the wrong thing. The songs are the legacy. The seats are just seats.

Ten Years Old and Building a Catalog, Not Just a Following
The drummers getting the most attention on YouTube right now skew young, and the good ones aren't just doing viral drum-offs — they're building actual bodies of work. That's the real signal to watch, not the view count on any single clip. A kid with a following and no catalog fades. A kid building original material while the following is still growing has a career, not a moment. The drum-streaming generation coming up now has tools nobody had a decade ago — instant global distribution, direct fan access, zero gatekeepers. What they do with the next five years of that access matters more than any single viral clip does right now.
