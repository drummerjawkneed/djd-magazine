A drummer from Hamilton, Alabama just won $25,000 for playing drums in front of people. Jonathan Fortson took the Guitar Center Drum-Off 2026 title, the first run of that competition in a decade. Ten years is a long time to leave a stage empty. In that gap, a generation of drummers learned their craft through screens, and plenty of them built audiences of hundreds from a bedroom with a camera pointed at the kit. Now the old-school national bracket is back, and it crowned a champion in a world that looks nothing like 2016.

That's the thread running through this issue. Modern Drummer named Celeste Fox the 2026 winner of the Neil Peart Spirit of Drumming Scholarship, a reminder that the next wave is already working. Stephen Perkins gets three separate kit deep-dives, from the 1992 Porno for Pyros setup to a Jimmy Page-inspired DW monster built for The Panic Channel. Each one is a lesson in choosing your sound instead of inheriting it. And Eric Singer is putting a Drum Off on a KISS cruise ship, because of course he is.

Competition, scholarship, gear obsession, floating rock and roll. Drummers keep finding new rooms to fill. Some of those rooms are ballrooms. Some are a spare bedroom at 11PM with a stream running. Both count. Read on, then go hit something with intention.

The Drum-Off Is Back, and Alabama Owns It

SourceModern Drummer Magazine · Sep 10, 2026

Guitar Center announced Jonathan Fortson of Hamilton, Alabama as the Drum-Off 2026 Champion, closing out the first run of the legendary national competition in a decade. Fortson takes home the title, brand endorsements, professional gear, and a $25,000 cash prize.

The money is real, but the timing matters more. The Drum-Off went quiet for ten years while drumming moved onto phones, livestreams, and short clips. A player can now build an audience without ever entering a contest. So why does a national bracket still matter? Because a live, judged, high-pressure stage tests something a polished clip can't: what you do when nobody can hit retake.

Fortson's win puts a small-town Alabama drummer in front of the industry with gear and endorsements behind him. That is what these competitions were always for. Whether the Drum-Off sticks around after this return is Guitar Center's call to make. Drummers should keep showing up regardless.

Celeste Fox Wins 2026 Neil Peart Spirit of Drumming Scholarship
Modern Drummer, Universal Music Enterprises (UMe), Carrie Peart (Trustee of The Estate of Neil Peart), and Olivia Peart named Celeste Fox the 2026 recipient. The scholarship was established to honor Neil Peart by inspiring young drummers. Scholarships like this do quiet, useful work: they back a player before the industry does. Peart spent a career treating the kit as something worth studying. Attaching his name to a young drummer's future is a fair way to keep that going.

Stephen Perkins Built a Jimmy Page-Inspired DW Kit for The Panic Channel
When Stephen Perkins formed The Panic Channel, he wanted to step away from the fast, tom-heavy language of Jane's Addiction and Porno for Pyros. His answer was a massive custom DW kit inspired by Jimmy Page, John Bonham, and the physical power of 1970s rock. It's a useful lesson for anyone chasing a new sound: sometimes the fix is a bigger, heavier kit and fewer notes.

Perkins' 1997 Jane's Addiction Relapse Kit: Built for Speed and Clarity
Six years after Jane's Addiction broke apart, Stephen Perkins returned to the band's music with Flea on bass and a new rhythm-section chemistry to build. His tool was a radically different DW Short Stack kit designed for speed, articulation, and clarity. A reunion is easy to coast through, so a new kit for a new chemistry says something about how seriously he took it.

The 1992 Porno for Pyros Kit That Gave Perkins a New Voice
After Jane's Addiction, Stephen Perkins wasn't looking to repeat himself. His one-of-one 1992 DW Porno for Pyros kit became the instrument that helped him rethink his sound, translate hand percussion to the drum set, and begin again. The kit wasn't a trophy. It was a tool for starting over, and that's worth remembering next time you feel stuck in your own habits.

Three Nights a Week, One Electronic Kit, Four Platforms

DrummerJawkneeD streams Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday at 11PM EST on a Roland TD-50, sending the show to Twitch, Kick, YouTube, and X at the same time. That schedule is the point. Late-night drumming, three times a week, no matter how the last stream went. Consistency is the least glamorous part of streaming and the part that matters most.

The honest take: the electronic kit is a choice, not a limit. It lets the stream stay clean and the late-night volume stay reasonable, and the setup around it keeps growing more ambitious. The next thing to watch is whether the streams keep tightening up. Better transitions, cleaner production, and a stronger show flow are the difference between drumming on camera and running a show.

Drum Culture · 01
The Stage Never Left. It Just Got Quieter.
The Drum-Off took a ten-year break. Nobody noticed at first because drummers kept playing. They played on phones, on streams, on comment-section challenges, on loops of a fill posted at 2AM. The stage moved to a screen. But a screen has no judges and no room. When Jonathan Fortson won in Alabama-to-national fashion, it reminded everyone that live, timed, watched-in-person playing still separates the drummers who can from the drummers who can edit. That isn't an insult to online players. It's a different skill. The best ones now do both. If you only ever play for a camera, enter something live this year. A local showcase, an open mic, a school gym. Let the room push back. You'll learn more in one nervous song than in fifty clean takes.
02
Drum Culture · 02
Scholarships Are a Quiet Kind of Rock Star Move
Celeste Fox gets the Neil Peart scholarship. Somebody will read that and shrug because there's no drama in it. No walkout, no viral clip. That's exactly why it matters. Music culture loves the loud story and skips the slow one, but the slow one is how the craft survives. A scholarship means a young drummer gets time, lessons, and a bit of belief from people who know what it takes. Peart's name on it isn't decoration. He treated the kit as a lifelong study, and this keeps that attitude alive. If you're an established player with any reach at all, find one young drummer to help this year. A free lesson, a spare pair of sticks, an honest critique. It costs almost nothing.
03
Drum Culture · 03
Your Kit Is a Statement, So Make One
Three stories this month cover three Stephen Perkins kits, and the through-line is obvious. Each setup answered a question about who he wanted to be next. Tom-heavy and fast for one era. Clear and articulate for a reunion. Big, heavy, and Bonham-flavored for a rock band. Most of us buy gear the opposite way: a review, a sale, a friend's opinion. Try flipping it. Before you add a cymbal, decide what sound you're missing. Before you shrink your kit, decide what you're tired of hitting. A kit is a set of decisions you can hear. If yours doesn't sound like anything in particular, that's not a gear problem. It's a choice you haven't made yet.
04
Drum Culture · 04
A Drum Battle on a Cruise Ship Is the Right Idea
Eric Singer's Drum Off happens on the KISS Kruise, in a ballroom, at midday on a Sunday, in front of the band's own drummer. Some people will roll their eyes. Don't. Drumming has always had a ridiculous side, and the best scenes embrace it. Nobody signs up for this because it's dignified. They sign up because it's a story they'll tell for ten years. The sport-style format works, too. Short slot, live audience, judges who actually play. It pushes people to commit to something instead of noodling for the camera. More of this, please. Put drummers in strange rooms, hand them a timer, and see who shows up. The polished streams will still be there afterward.
